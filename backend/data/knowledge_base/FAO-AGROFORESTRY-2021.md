# Agroforestry and Dryland Restoration: Carbon Sequestration and Biodiversity Co-benefits

**Authoring Organization**: Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO)  
**Publication Year**: 2021  
**Identifier**: FAO Technical Guide 2021, ISBN 978-92-5-134230-5  
**Biome Applicability**: Semi-arid, Arid, Tropical Savanna, Mediterranean Drylands  
**Target Ecological Domains**: Soil Organic Carbon, Microclimate Regulation, Pollinator Biodiversity  

---

## Executive Summary & Core Findings

Dryland agroforestry systems incorporating *Faidherbia albida* (winter thorn / apple-ring acacia) and native leguminous tree parklands represent a foundational ecological restoration intervention across water-limited drylands. *Faidherbia albida* exhibits reverse phenology: it sheds nutrient-dense foliage at the onset of the wet cropping season, avoiding canopy light competition with adjacent cereal crops while depositing 2.5 to 3.5 tonnes of nitrogen-rich leaf litter per hectare annually.

### Key Quantitative Benchmarks
- **Soil Carbon Sequestration**: 0.35 to 0.55 tonnes of organic carbon per hectare per year (t C/ha/yr) in the top 0–30 cm soil layer over a 5 to 10-year horizon.
- **Wind Velocity & Surface Temperature**: Wind speed is attenuated by 40% to 60% within 15 tree-height distances leeward of established shelterbelts. Topsoil temperature extremes are moderated by 4°C to 7°C, reducing boundary-layer vapor pressure deficit (VPD) and non-productive evaporative water loss.
- **Crop Yield Stabilization**: Multi-site trials across semi-arid cereals (sorghum, pearl millet, dryland wheat) demonstrated a 25% to 35% improvement in grain yield resilience compared to continuous bare-fallow monocultures during drought seasons.
- **Biological Corridors**: Native dryland tree canopies increase arthropod and wild avian species richness by over 160%, providing essential nesting sites and floral stepping stones across fragmented landscapes.
